## What I'm asking for

A loan of **$50,000**, on paper, with the car as the collateral. Not a gift. Not a share of anything. A loan with a date it comes back, interest, and a brake that stops it from moving until two things exist.

Total: about **$40,000–$50,000**. Your $50,000 is one car and its first ninety nights, with a cushion. That is the whole first move. Nothing else on any list I've written needs your money.

## Why you, why now

- **Nobody else lends on this yet.** Banks lend on buildings and on businesses with two years of books. We have neither. The state's grant program says in its own rules that it won't buy vehicles, and its next application window opens in February 2027. So in 2026 the only money that can put a car at a bar door is ours — and I don't have $25,000 liquid. You have the $50,000.
- **The car is the proof.** Once one car has ninety nights of logs and the state has written down that the crashes moved, everything else becomes fundable by other people's money — the state's, a bank's, a foundation's. Your $50,000 is the only dollar in the whole plan that has to come before the proof exists.
- **It's a real problem, in the state's own numbers.** Salt Lake County had 380 drunk-driving crashes in 2024, 30 of them with a death, and 145 of the 380 happened between 10 p.m. and 3 a.m. The state's own plan says the men it's trying to reach "don't know their sober-ride choices." A car that is reliably at the door *is* the choice.

## What protects your money

1. **The brake.** Not one dollar leaves your account until (a) the state's highway safety office has signed a one-page agreement saying how it will measure whether this worked, and (b) the company's bank account exists. Both are in the note itself, not just on this page.
2. **The car is yours if it fails.** The note is secured by the car's title. If the whole thing stops, you hold a car worth most of what you lent, and you sell it.
3. **You get the number.** Every three months the state gets a report against its own yardstick. You get the same number, the same day.
4. **Your name is on paper, not on a company.** You are a lender. You are not an owner, not a board member unless you want to be, not liable for anything the company does.
5. **The order can't be skipped.** Company filed ($59). Nonprofit filed ($59). The state's question emailed ($0). Two bars in writing ($0). *Then* the note. *Then* the car. I can't jump ahead, because the note won't let me.


## One more thing the founders will see, so you see it first

You're inside the founder family — A.A₂ on their page. That makes this a loan from a founder's family to the founders' company. Plain words for what that changes and what it doesn't:

- **It changes nothing about the money.** Same note, same collateral, same brake, same number every three months.
- **It changes one line on paper:** the note says, in one sentence, that the lender is related to a founder, and the interest is a rate a bank would charge — so nobody can later say the company did its own family a favor, or that you did.
- **It changes who has to see it before signing:** all three founders, in writing. Not because anything's hidden — because a related-party loan that's written down is ordinary, and one that isn't is the kind of thing that sinks a small company in an audit or a divorce.
- **It doesn't make you an owner.** Lender stays lender. If you ever want a share instead, that's a different piece of paper, and the founders would have to write it.

## What this is not

- **Not the building.** The Midvale property — six twin homes, listed for $5.9 million — is a separate question with separate money (a seller's carry or a bank), and it's not a 2026 question. Your money never touches it.
- **Not stock.** Not Tesla, not SpaceX, not anything I buy on my own account. Those are my money and my risk.
- **Not the detox center, the elderly rides, the medicine runs, the satellites.** Those are later pages, each behind its own gate. None of them opens until the first car has its ninety nights.

- **"What if nobody rides?"** The rider isn't the customer. The state and the bars are. If the state won't sign the yardstick, the brake holds and your money never moves.
- **"You can't even drive it."** Not until 2033. I can own it, log it, and be measured on it. The car has a log, the note has a date, the state has the number.
- **"How do I know any of this is real?"** Every line has a public page or a receipt. The binder is the proof, and you hold the note.
- **"When do I get it back?"** On the date in the note — and sooner if the state's grant or a bank replaces it, which is the plan.
